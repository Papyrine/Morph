## Location

<table style="width:100%;">
<colgroup>

<td><p>Remember that location is of paramount importance to some types
of businesses, less so for others.</p>
<ul>
<li><p>If your business does not require specific location
considerations, that could be an advantage, and you should note it
here.</p></li>
<li><p>If you have already chosen your location, describe the
highlights—you can use some of the factors outlined in the next bullet
as a guide or other factors that are essential considerations for your
business.</p></li>
<li><p>If you do not yet have a location, describe the key criteria for
determining a suitable location for your business.</p></li>
</ul>
<p>Consider the following examples (note that this is not an exhaustive
list, and you might have other considerations as well):</p>
<p>What kind of space are you seeking and where? Is there a particular
area that would be especially desirable from a marketing viewpoint? Must
you have a ground-floor location? If so, must your location be easily
accessible by public transportation?</p>
<p>If you are considering a specific site or comparing sites, the
following may be important: How is the access/traffic flow? Are the
parking facilities adequate? Is the street lighting sufficient? Is it
close to other businesses or venues that might aid in drawing the type
of customers you seek? If it is a storefront, does it attract attention
or what must be done to make it attract the type of attention you
need?</p>
<p>If signage is appropriate for your business: Are there local
ordinances concerning signs that might adversely affect you? What type
of signage would best serve your needs? Have you included the cost of
signage in your start-up figures?</p></td>
</tr>

## Income project statement

<table>
<caption>Income projection statement table</caption>
<colgroup>
<col style="width: 4%" />
<col style="width: 95%" />
</colgroup>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><img src="media/image2.svg"
style="width:0.2in;height:0.2in" alt="Play with solid fill" /></td>
<td><p>The Income Projection Statement is another management tool to
preview the amount of income generated each month based on reasonable
predictions of the monthly level of sales and costs/expenses. As the
monthly projections are developed and entered, these figures serve as
goals to control operating expenses. As actual results occur, a
comparison with the predicted amounts should produce warning bells if
costs are getting out of line so that steps can be taken to correct
problems.</p>
<p>The Industrial Percentage (Ind. %) is calculated by multiplying
costs/expenses by 100% and dividing the result by total net sales. It
indicates the total sales that are standard for a particular industry.
You may be able to get this information from trade associations,
accountants, banks, or reference libraries. Industry figures are a
useful benchmark against which to compare the costs/expenses of your own
business. Compare your annual percentage with the figure indicated in
the industry percentage column.</p>
<p>The following is an explanation for some of the terms used in the
table that follows:</p>
<ul>
<li><p>Total Net Sales (Revenue): This figure is your total estimated
sales per month. Be as realistic as possible, considering seasonal
trends, returns, allowances, and markdowns.</p></li>
<li><p>Cost of Sales: To be realistic, this figure must include all the
costs involved in making a sale. For example, where inventory is
concerned, include the cost of transportation and shipping. Any direct
labor cost should also be included.</p></li>
<li><p>Gross Profit: Subtract the cost of sales from the total net
sales.</p></li>
<li><p>Gross Profit Margin: This is calculated by dividing gross profits
by total net sales.</p></li>
<li><p>Controllable Expenses: Salaries (base plus overtime), payroll
expenses (including paid vacations, sick leave, health insurance,
unemployment insurance and social security taxes), cost of outside
services (including subcontracts, overflow work and special or one-time
services), supplies (including all items and services purchased for use
in the business), utilities (water, heat, light, trash collection,
etc.), repair and maintenance (including both regular and periodic
expenses, such as painting), advertising, travel and auto (including
business use of personal car, parking, and business trips), accounting
and legal (the cost of outside professional services).</p></li>
<li><p>Fixed Expenses: Rent (only for real estate used in business),
depreciation (the amortization of capital assets), insurance (fire,
liability on property or products, workers’ compensation, theft, etc.),
loan repayments (include the interest and principal payments on
outstanding loans to the business), miscellaneous (unspecified, small
expenditures not included under other accounts or headings).</p></li>
<li><p>Net Profit/Loss (Before Taxes): Subtract total expenses from
gross profit.</p></li>
<li><p>Taxes: Inventory, sales, excise, real estate, federal, state,
etc.</p></li>
<li><p>Net Profit/Loss (After Taxes): Subtract taxes from net profit
before taxes.</p></li>
<li><p>Annual Total: Add all monthly figures across the table for each
sale and expense item.</p></li>
<li><p>Annual Percentage: Multiply the annual total by 100% and divide
the result by the total net sales figure. Compare to industry percentage
in first column.</p></li>
</ul></td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>

## Balance sheet

<table>
<caption>Balance sheet table</caption>
<colgroup>
<col style="width: 6%" />
<col style="width: 93%" />
</colgroup>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><img src="media/image2.svg"
style="width:0.2in;height:0.2in" alt="Play with solid fill" /></td>
<td><p>Following are guidelines for what to include in the balance
sheet: (For use in established businesses)</p>
<ul>
<li><p>Assets: Anything of value that is owned or is legally due to a
business. Total assets include all net values; the amounts that result
from subtracting depreciation and amortization from the original cost
when the asset was first acquired.</p></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Current assets</strong></p>
<p>Cash—Money in the bank or resources that can be converted into cash
within 12 months of the date of the balance sheet.</p>
<ul>
<li><p>Petty Cash—A fund of cash for small, miscellaneous
expenditures.</p></li>
<li><p>Accounts Receivable—Amounts due from clients for merchandise or
services.</p></li>
<li><p>Inventory—Raw materials on hand, work-in-progress, and all
finished goods (either manufactured or purchased for resale).</p></li>
<li><p>Short-term Investments—Interest or dividend-yielding holdings
expected to be converted to cash within a year; stocks, bonds,
certificates of deposit and time-deposit savings accounts. These should
be shown at either their cost or current market value, whichever is
less. Short-term investments may also be called “temporary investments”
or “marketable securities.”</p></li>
<li><p>Prepaid Expense—Goods, benefits, or services that a business pays
for or rents in advance, such as office supplies, insurance, or
workspace.</p></li>
<li><p>Long-term Investments—Holdings that a business intends to retain
for at least a year. Also known as long-term assets, these are usually
interest or dividend paying stocks, bonds, or savings accounts.</p></li>
<li><p>Fixed Assets—This term includes all resources that a business
owns or acquires for use in its operations that are not intended for
resale. They may be leased rather than owned and, depending upon the
leasing arrangements, may have to be included both as an asset for the
value and as a liability. Fixed assets include land (the original
purchase price should be listed, without allowance for market value),
buildings, improvements, equipment, furniture, vehicles.</p></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Liabilities:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><p>Current Liabilities: Include all debts, monetary obligations, and
claims payable within 12 months.</p></li>
<li><p>Accounts Payable—Amounts due to suppliers for goods and services
purchased for the business.</p></li>
<li><p>Notes Payable—The balance of the principal due on short-term
debt, funds borrowed for the business. Also includes the current amount
due on notes whose terms exceed 12 months.</p></li>
<li><p>Interest Payable—Accrued amounts due on both short and long-term
borrowed capital and credit extended to the business.</p></li>
<li><p>Taxes Payable—Amounts incurred during the accounting period
covered by the balance sheet.</p></li>
<li><p>Payroll Accrual—Salaries and wages owed during the period covered
by the balance sheet.</p></li>
<li><p>Long-term Liabilities—Notes, contract payments, or mortgage
payments due over a period exceeding 12 months. These should be listed
by outstanding balance less the current position due.</p></li>
<li><p>Net Worth—Also called owner’s equity. This is the amount of the
claim of the owner(s) on the assets of the business. In a proprietorship
or partnership, this equity is each owner’s original investment plus any
earnings after withdrawals.</p></li>
</ul>
<p>Most computerized bookkeeping systems can generate a balance sheet
for the period(s) required.</p>
<p>Note: Total assets will always equal total liabilities plus total net
worth. That is, the bottom-line figures for total assets and total
liabilities will always be the same.</p></td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>

## Break-even analysis

<table>
<colgroup>
<col style="width: 6%" />
<col style="width: 93%" />
</colgroup>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><img src="media/image2.svg"
style="width:0.2in;height:0.2in" alt="Play with solid fill" /></td>
<td><p>Use this section to evaluate your business profitability. You can
measure how close you are to achieving that break-even point when your
expenses are covered by the amount of your sales and are on the brink of
profitability.</p>
<p>A break-even analysis can tell you what sales volume you are going to
need to generate a profit. It can also be used as a guide for setting
prices.</p>
<p>There are three basic ways to increase the profits of your business:
generate more sales, raise prices, and/or lower costs. All can affect
your business: if you raise prices, you may no longer be competitive; if
you generate more sales, you may need added personnel to service those
sales which would increase your costs. Lowering the fixed costs your
business must pay each month will have a greater impact on the profit
margin than changing variable costs.</p>
<ul>
<li><p>Fixed costs: Rent, insurance, salaries, etc.</p></li>
<li><p>Variable costs: The cost at which you buy products, supplies,
etc.</p></li>
<li><p>Contribution Margin: This is the selling price minus the variable
costs. It measures the dollars available to pay the fixed costs and make
a profit.</p></li>
<li><p>Contribution Margin Ratio: This is the number of total sales
minus the variable costs, divided by total sales. It measures the
percentage of each sales dollar to pay fixed costs and make a
profit.</p></li>
<li><p>Break-even Point: This is the amount when the total sales equal
the total expenses. It is the minimum sales dollar you need to reach
before you make a profit.</p></li>
<li><p>Break-even Point in Units: For applicable businesses, this is the
total of fixed costs divided by the unit selling price minus the
variable costs per unit. It tells you how many units you need to sell
before you make a profit.</p></li>
<li><p>Break-even Point in Dollars: This is the total amount of fixed
costs divided by the contribution margin ratio. It is a method of
calculating the minimum sales dollar to reach before you make a
profit.</p></li>
<li><p>Note: If the sales dollars are below the break-even point, your
business is losing money.</p></li>
</ul></td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
